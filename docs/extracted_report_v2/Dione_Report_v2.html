ABSTRACT

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has significantly enhanced the ability of artificial intelligence systems to understand, generate, and reason over human language. Models such as GPT-4, LLaMA, and Mistral have demonstrated impressive performance across a wide range of tasks, including text generation, summarization, translation, and complex reasoning. However, these models remain fundamentally limited in their functionality as they operate primarily within isolated chat interfaces. While they can suggest solutions or generate outputs, they lack the capability to execute real-world digital actions, creating a critical limitation known as the Actuation Gap. This disconnect restricts AI systems from transitioning beyond passive assistants to truly autonomous agents that can independently carry out tasks on behalf of the user.

To address this limitation, this project introduces Dione, a Local Large Action Model (LAM) Orchestration Engine designed to enable secure and autonomous execution of tasks. Unlike existing cloud-based agentic AI systems such as AutoGPT and LangChain agents that require sensitive user data to be transmitted to external servers, Dione operates entirely on the user’s local device. It acts as an intelligent middleware that bridges the reasoning capabilities of local LLMs with system-level tools, APIs, and applications. By converting unstructured model outputs into structured, executable commands, Dione enables AI to perform multi-step workflows such as retrieving data from local file systems, processing information through various pipelines, sending emails via Gmail API, managing calendar events, and triggering actions across different platforms and applications.

The proposed system emphasizes a privacy-first, modular, and secure architecture built on principles such as zero-trust local inference, sandboxed plugin execution, and complete data sovereignty. Through its micro-kernel design and ReAct-based operational loop, Dione ensures efficient task execution while maintaining strict control over user data. The system incorporates a Context Manager powered by ChromaDB for maintaining conversational memory, a Model Adapter for seamless integration with multiple LLM backends, a Plugin Registry for dynamic tool management, and a Safety Guardrail module for preventing unauthorized or dangerous operations. This project demonstrates that it is possible to build powerful agentic AI systems without compromising privacy, thereby paving the way for the next generation of intelligent, user-controlled digital assistants.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

This chapter introduces the concept, background, and motivation behind the proposed system, Dione – a Local Large Action Model (LAM) Orchestration Engine. It highlights the limitations of existing AI systems, defines the problem being addressed, establishes the relevance of the project in the current technological landscape, and outlines the methodology adopted to develop a secure, privacy-first agentic AI system. The chapter provides the necessary context for understanding the significance of the project and sets the stage for the detailed technical discussions that follow in subsequent chapters.

1.1 Introduction to the Project

The rapid evolution of Artificial Intelligence over the past decade has led to the development of Large Language Models (LLMs) capable of performing advanced reasoning, natural language understanding, and content generation at unprecedented levels of sophistication. Models such as OpenAI’s GPT-4, Meta’s LLaMA 2, and Mistral 7B have demonstrated the ability to engage in complex conversations, answer domain-specific questions, generate code, summarize lengthy documents, and even perform mathematical and logical reasoning tasks with remarkable accuracy. These advancements have been made possible through innovations in transformer architectures, attention mechanisms, and large-scale pre-training on diverse text corpora.

Despite these impressive advancements, such models are inherently limited in their functionality as they operate within isolated environments, primarily restricted to chat-based interactions. A user may ask an AI model to draft an email, organize files, or schedule a meeting, and the model can provide detailed instructions or generate the required text. However, the model itself cannot perform the action – it cannot open the email client, compose and send the email, navigate the file system, or interact with the calendar application. This fundamental limitation creates what researchers have termed the “Actuation Gap” – the disconnect between an AI system’s ability to reason about actions and its ability to actually execute them in the real world.

To overcome this limitation, the proposed system, Dione, is designed as a Local Large Action Model (LAM) Orchestration Engine. It acts as an intermediary layer that bridges the gap between the reasoning capabilities of LLMs and the execution capabilities of system-level tools and applications. The name “Dione” is inspired by the moon of Saturn, symbolizing a system that orbits around and augments a larger body – in this case, the LLM. By translating unstructured outputs from the model into structured, executable commands using JSON schemas, Dione enables the automation of real-world digital tasks. These tasks include but are not limited to retrieving and manipulating files on the local file system, composing and sending emails through the Gmail API, reading and responding to messages on WhatsApp, managing calendar events, executing system commands, and interacting with web browsers and other applications.

The system is designed to operate entirely on the user’s local device, eliminating the need for cloud-based processing and ensuring complete data privacy. This local-first approach is a key differentiator from existing agentic AI solutions such as AutoGPT, BabyAGI, and LangChain-based agents, which typically rely on cloud APIs and require users to share sensitive credentials and personal data with external servers. By keeping all data and processing local, Dione provides users with full control over their information and reduces the attack surface for potential security breaches.

1.2 Motivation for the Project

The primary motivation behind this project arises from the inherent limitation of current AI systems, which are capable of reasoning but incapable of acting. This creates a dependency on users to manually execute tasks even after receiving accurate guidance from AI models. For instance, a user might ask an AI assistant to help organize their schedule for the week. The AI can generate a detailed plan, but the user must then manually enter each event into their calendar application, send reminder emails to participants, and set up necessary documents – a process that is time-consuming and defeats the purpose of having an AI assistant in the first place.

Additionally, existing agentic AI systems that attempt to bridge this gap rely heavily on cloud-based architectures. Systems like AutoGPT and various LangChain-based agents require users to provide API keys for services like OpenAI, which means that every interaction, every piece of personal data, and every command passes through external servers. This introduces significant concerns related to privacy, security, and data ownership. Users must trust that their sensitive information – including email contents, personal documents, financial data, and authentication credentials – will be handled responsibly by third-party service providers. Recent incidents of data breaches and unauthorized data usage by AI companies have further amplified these concerns.

Furthermore, the reliance on cloud infrastructure introduces several practical limitations. Internet connectivity becomes a prerequisite for any AI-assisted task, making the system unreliable in environments with poor or no network access. Latency issues arise from the round-trip time required to send data to remote servers and receive processed results. Cloud-based services often impose rate limits and usage quotas that restrict the number and frequency of API calls, limiting the system’s throughput. Subscription costs for premium AI services create financial barriers that may exclude individual users and small organizations from accessing advanced AI capabilities.

The motivation of this project is therefore to develop a local-first AI system that not only performs intelligent reasoning but also executes tasks autonomously while ensuring complete data privacy and user control. Dione aims to demonstrate that it is possible to build a fully functional agentic AI system that operates without any dependency on cloud infrastructure, using only locally deployed language models and locally executed plugins. This approach aligns with the growing movement toward edge computing and decentralized AI, where processing occurs closer to the data source rather than in centralized cloud data centers.

1.3 Drawbacks of Existing Systems

Current implementations of AI-driven assistants and agent frameworks suffer from several critical limitations that collectively hinder their adoption and effectiveness. A thorough analysis of existing systems reveals the following key drawbacks that the proposed system, Dione, seeks to address.

First, most existing systems are cloud-dependent, requiring all user data to be transmitted to and processed on remote servers. This architectural choice means that sensitive information such as personal emails, documents, browsing history, and authentication tokens are exposed to third-party infrastructure during processing. Even with encryption in transit, data is typically decrypted on the server side for processing, creating windows of vulnerability. High-profile data breaches at major technology companies have demonstrated that even sophisticated security measures cannot guarantee the protection of user data stored on remote servers.

Second, these systems often require the storage of high-privilege credentials such as API tokens, OAuth keys, and service account passwords on centralized platforms. This concentration of authentication credentials makes these platforms attractive targets for cyberattacks. A single breach can compromise the credentials of thousands or millions of users simultaneously. The practice of storing credentials on third-party servers also raises questions about data governance and compliance with regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA).

Third, the dependency on internet connectivity introduces latency and reduces system reliability. In scenarios where network conditions are poor or unavailable, cloud-dependent AI assistants become completely non-functional. This limitation is particularly problematic for users in remote areas, those working in secure environments that restrict internet access, and situations where network disruptions occur due to natural disasters or infrastructure failures. The round-trip time required for cloud processing also adds noticeable delay to interactions, degrading the user experience.

Fourth, existing systems lack true autonomy in task execution. While they can handle simple, single-step operations such as answering a question or generating text, they struggle with complex, multi-step workflows that require sequential actions, conditional logic, and error handling. For example, a task like “find the most recent invoice in my downloads folder, extract the amount due, and send a reminder email to the vendor” requires multiple coordinated actions across different applications, with each step dependent on the successful completion of the previous one. Current systems typically lack the orchestration capabilities needed to manage such workflows reliably.

Fifth, vendor-imposed restrictions and alignment policies further limit the flexibility and usability of cloud-based AI systems. Many commercial AI providers implement content filtering, usage policies, and output restrictions that prevent the model from performing certain types of tasks or generating certain types of content. While these restrictions serve important safety purposes, they can also prevent legitimate use cases and reduce the system’s utility for power users who need unrestricted access to AI capabilities for professional or research purposes.

1.4 Problem Definition

The problem addressed in this project is the inability of current AI systems to bridge the gap between reasoning and execution while maintaining user privacy and system security. Specifically, the project identifies and addresses the following core challenges.

The primary challenge is the Actuation Gap – the fundamental disconnect between an AI model’s ability to understand and reason about tasks and its ability to actually execute those tasks in the user’s digital environment. Current LLMs can generate detailed plans, write code, and provide step-by-step instructions, but they cannot interact with the user’s operating system, applications, or services to carry out the planned actions. This gap significantly limits the practical utility of AI assistants and creates a bottleneck in human-AI collaboration workflows.

The secondary challenge is the Privacy-Security Paradox – the conflict between the need for AI systems to access sensitive user data in order to perform useful tasks and the need to protect that data from unauthorized access, misuse, or exposure. Existing cloud-based solutions require users to sacrifice privacy in exchange for functionality, creating a trade-off that many users and organizations find unacceptable, particularly in privacy-sensitive domains such as healthcare, finance, legal services, and government operations.

The system must therefore be designed to interpret natural language instructions from the user, convert them into structured actions using well-defined schemas, and execute them directly within the user’s local environment without transmitting any data to external servers. The system must operate entirely on local hardware to eliminate reliance on cloud infrastructure, ensuring data sovereignty and reducing latency. Additionally, it should provide a secure execution mechanism that validates all actions before execution, prevents unintended or harmful operations, and supports scalable integration with various tools and applications through a modular plugin architecture.

The proposed system is highly relevant in the current technological landscape, where several converging trends are driving demand for the type of solution that Dione provides. The growing shift towards Agentic AI represents a fundamental evolution in how AI systems are designed and deployed. Unlike traditional chatbot-style AI that simply responds to queries, agentic AI systems are designed to take autonomous actions on behalf of the user, effectively serving as digital agents that can independently plan, execute, and monitor complex tasks. Industry analysts predict that agentic AI will be one of the most transformative technology trends of the next decade, with applications spanning personal productivity, enterprise automation, scientific research, and creative work.

This trend toward edge AI reduces reliance on cloud infrastructure, improves response times, and enhances data privacy.

Dione addresses these trends by enabling autonomous task execution without exposing sensitive data to external entities. It is particularly relevant for applications in domains such as personal productivity, where users need AI assistance with daily tasks like email management, scheduling, and document organization; enterprise automation, where businesses need to automate repetitive workflows while maintaining control over proprietary data; and privacy-sensitive sectors including finance, healthcare, and legal services, where regulatory requirements mandate strict data handling practices. The project aligns with the future direction of AI systems that prioritize decentralization, security, and user ownership, making it a timely and significant contribution to the field.

1.6 Methodology Used

The development of Dione follows a modular and structured approach based on a micro-kernel architecture, which separates the core orchestration logic from the various functional modules that handle specific aspects of the system’s operation. This architectural choice ensures flexibility, scalability, and ease of maintenance, as individual modules can be developed, tested, and updated independently without affecting the rest of the system.

The system separates the reasoning and execution components into distinct layers. The reasoning layer is responsible for understanding user intent, generating action plans, and making decisions about which tools to use and in what order. The execution layer is responsible for translating these plans into concrete actions, invoking the appropriate plugins, and handling the results. This separation of concerns allows each layer to be optimized independently and enables the system to support multiple LLM backends and execution environments.

A ReAct (Reason + Act) loop is implemented as the core operational paradigm, allowing the system to iteratively reason about a task, perform an action, observe the result, and refine its approach based on the outcome. This iterative approach is particularly well-suited for complex, multi-step tasks where the optimal course of action may not be known in advance and must be discovered through trial and refinement. The ReAct loop draws inspiration from the seminal work by Yao et al. (2023), which demonstrated that combining reasoning and acting in a single loop significantly improves the performance of language model agents on complex tasks.

The system integrates local LLMs through frameworks such as Ollama and Llama.cpp, enabling on-device inference without any network dependency. Context management is achieved using Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) techniques with a vector database powered by ChromaDB to maintain conversational memory, store user preferences, and improve response accuracy over time. Task execution is handled through a plugin-based architecture, where each tool – such as the Gmail plugin, WhatsApp plugin, file system plugin, and calendar plugin – operates as an independent module with a well-defined interface and sandboxed execution environment.

To ensure system reliability and security, validation mechanisms using libraries such as Pydantic are incorporated to verify the structure and content of all generated commands before execution. Permission-based execution controls ensure that sensitive operations require explicit user approval, while sandboxing techniques isolate plugin execution from the core system to prevent malicious or erroneous actions from causing system-wide damage. This comprehensive methodology ensures that the system remains robust, secure, and efficient while performing complex multi-step operations in a privacy-preserving manner.

2.1 Research Papers

A thorough review of recent academic publications was conducted to understand the current state of the art in agentic AI, tool-augmented language models, and privacy-preserving AI systems. The following papers were identified as the most relevant to the design and development of Dione, and each paper was analyzed for its contributions, methodologies, and limitations.

2.1.1 Paper 1: Toolformer – Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools (2023)

Abstract: This landmark paper by Schick et al. introduces Toolformer, a framework that enables language models to autonomously learn when and how to call external tools such as calculators, search engines, translation systems, and APIs during the inference process. The key innovation of Toolformer lies in its self-supervised training approach, where the model learns to insert API calls into its generated text based on whether those calls improve the quality of the output. The model is trained on a dataset where API calls are automatically annotated using a filtering mechanism that retains only those calls that reduce the model’s perplexity on subsequent tokens. This approach eliminates the need for manually annotated training data and allows the model to discover effective tool usage patterns on its own.

Inference Drawn: Toolformer represents a significant step forward in enabling language models to interact with external tools and services. Its self-supervised approach to learning tool usage is elegant and scalable, demonstrating that language models can be taught to augment their capabilities through tool integration. However, Toolformer has several limitations that are directly relevant to Dione’s design. First, it does not provide a complete execution pipeline – while the model can generate API calls, there is no orchestration layer that manages the execution of these calls, handles errors, or coordinates multi-step workflows. Second, Toolformer lacks security and validation mechanisms, meaning that generated API calls are executed without any verification of their safety or correctness. Third, Toolformer is typically used in cloud environments, raising the same privacy concerns that Dione seeks to address. These limitations informed the design of Dione’s validation layer and its emphasis on secure, controlled execution.

2.1.2 Paper 2: ReAct – Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models (2023)

Abstract: The ReAct framework, proposed by Yao et al., introduces a novel paradigm that combines reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner within a single language model. In the ReAct approach, the model alternates between generating “thoughts” (reasoning steps that analyze the current situation and plan the next action) and “actions” (concrete operations such as searching for information, looking up data, or performing calculations). The model also generates “observations” based on the results of its actions, which feed back into the reasoning process. This think-act-observe loop enables the model to dynamically adjust its strategy based on intermediate results, making it particularly effective for complex, multi-step tasks that require both reasoning and information gathering.

Inference Drawn: The ReAct framework provides the strongest conceptual foundation for Dione’s operational paradigm. The interleaved reasoning and acting approach is directly applicable to Dione’s workflow, where the system must understand user intent, plan a sequence of actions, execute each action through appropriate plugins, observe the results, and adjust its plan if necessary. However, ReAct as presented in the original paper has several significant limitations. It is fundamentally a prompting strategy rather than a complete system – it defines a pattern for structuring model inputs and outputs but does not address the system-level challenges of actually executing actions in a real computing environment. ReAct lacks mechanisms for validating the safety and correctness of generated actions, does not include any security controls such as permission management or sandboxing, and does not address the challenge of maintaining conversational context across multiple interactions. Dione builds upon ReAct’s conceptual foundation while addressing all of these limitations through its micro-kernel architecture, validation layer, and context management system.

2.1.3 Paper 3: Generative Agents – Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior (2023)

Abstract: This groundbreaking paper by Park et al. demonstrates computational agents capable of simulating believable human behavior in a virtual environment. The system creates a “small town” populated by 25 generative agents, each with a unique identity, memories, and goals. The agents autonomously plan their daily activities, form opinions, engage in conversations with each other, and coordinate social events. The key innovation is the agent architecture, which includes three components: a memory stream that records all of the agent’s experiences, a retrieval mechanism that surfaces relevant memories based on recency, importance, and relevance, and a reflection module that enables the agent to form higher-level abstractions and insights from its raw experiences.

Inference Drawn: The concepts of memory management and contextual reasoning presented in this paper are highly relevant to Dione’s Context Manager module. The memory stream architecture inspired Dione’s approach to maintaining conversation history and user interaction logs, while the retrieval mechanism informed the design of the RAG-based context augmentation system. The reflection module’s ability to synthesize higher-level insights from raw experiences aligns with Dione’s goal of improving its performance over time through accumulated interaction data. However, the Generative Agents system is fundamentally simulation-focused – it operates in a virtual environment where actions have no real-world consequences. It does not address the challenges of executing actions in real computing environments, managing security and permissions, or preserving user privacy. These gaps represent the core challenges that Dione’s execution engine and safety guardrail modules are designed to address.

2.1.4 Paper 4: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for Knowledge-Intensive Tasks (2020)

Abstract: Lewis et al. introduce Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), a framework that combines the parametric knowledge stored in a pre-trained language model with non-parametric knowledge retrieved from an external document index. During inference, the model first queries a dense retrieval system to find relevant passages from a large corpus, then conditions its generation on both the input query and the retrieved passages. This approach enables the model to access up-to-date information without requiring retraining, reducing hallucination by grounding generation in retrieved evidence, and allowing the model to handle knowledge-intensive tasks that exceed the capacity of its parametric memory.

Inference Drawn: RAG is a critical component of Dione’s architecture, forming the foundation of the Context Manager’s memory retrieval system. In Dione, RAG is used to retrieve relevant information from the user’s past interactions, stored documents, and system logs to augment the LLM’s input with contextual information. This enables the system to provide personalized responses that take into account the user’s preferences, previous commands, and the current state of their digital environment. However, RAG as described in the original paper focuses solely on enhancing the quality of generated text – it does not contribute to action execution, security management, or system control. In Dione, RAG is extended beyond its original text-generation role to serve as the backbone of a context-aware decision-making system that informs not only what the system says but also what the system does.

2.1.5 Paper 5: SuperAgent – A Scalable Multi-Agent Framework for Autonomous Task Execution (2024)

Abstract: SuperAgent, proposed by Malsuare et al. at DY Patil International University, presents a scalable multi-agent framework for autonomous task execution using Large Language Models. The system demonstrates coordinated execution across distributed agents, where multiple specialized agents collaborate to complete complex workflows. Performance was measured through metrics including distributed task execution efficiency and coordination time, showing promising results for automated workflow management.

Inference Drawn: SuperAgent validates the viability of LLM-based autonomous task execution and provides useful benchmarks for evaluating Dione’s performance. The multi-agent coordination approach offers insights into how complex tasks can be decomposed and distributed across specialized modules. However, SuperAgent remains in an early experimental stage with limited benchmarks and faces generalization challenges. Dione addresses these limitations through its more mature plugin architecture and its focus on single-device execution rather than distributed multi-agent coordination.

Articles on Agentic AI published between 2023 and 2024, including comprehensive surveys by researchers at institutions worldwide, discuss the emerging paradigm of autonomous AI agents that can plan, reason, and act independently. These articles trace the evolution from simple chatbot-style interactions to sophisticated agent architectures capable of multi-step task execution. They highlight the growing interest in agentic AI systems across both academia and industry, while also noting the significant challenges related to safety, reliability, and privacy that remain unsolved – challenges that Dione directly addresses.

Industry documentation and technical blogs from LangChain and Ollama provided practical insights into tool integration patterns, local model deployment strategies, and orchestration techniques. The LangChain documentation was particularly useful for understanding how to design modular, composable agent architectures, while the Ollama documentation informed the implementation of Dione’s local LLM inference pipeline. These resources collectively highlight the shift toward agentic AI but reveal a strong dependency on cloud-based systems and APIs, reinforcing the need for a local-first solution like Dione that eliminates these dependencies.

2.3 Interaction with Domain Experts

Structured interactions were conducted with faculty members specializing in Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning, as well as professionals working in Software Systems and Cybersecurity, to validate the feasibility of the proposed approach and gather insights that informed the system’s design.

Expert feedback on the technical feasibility of local LLM deployment was highly encouraging. Faculty members noted that the rapid improvement in model quantization techniques, such as GPTQ and AWQ, combined with the increasing availability of consumer-grade GPUs with substantial VRAM, has made it increasingly practical to run sophisticated language models locally. Professionals in the field confirmed that tools like Ollama and Llama.cpp have significantly reduced the barrier to entry for local model deployment, making it accessible to developers without specialized infrastructure.

On the topic of security, experts emphasized that validation and safety layers are not optional but essential components of any agentic AI system. They recommended implementing multiple layers of defense, including input validation, output sanitization, action verification, and sandboxed execution environments. This feedback directly shaped the design of Dione’s Safety Guardrail module and its permission-based execution system. Experts also recommended adopting a “zero-trust” approach where every action is verified regardless of its source, which was incorporated into Dione’s architectural principles.

From a software architecture perspective, experts unanimously recommended a plugin-based architecture for maximum scalability and modularity. They noted that a well-designed plugin system allows new capabilities to be added without modifying the core system, enables third-party developers to extend the system’s functionality, and simplifies testing and maintenance by isolating each plugin’s logic. This architectural recommendation was adopted as a fundamental design principle for Dione’s Plugin Registry module.

Finally, experts highlighted the strong and growing industry demand for privacy-preserving AI systems, particularly in sectors such as healthcare, finance, and government. They noted that regulatory frameworks like GDPR and HIPAA create legal requirements for data protection that cloud-based AI systems struggle to meet. 4.1 Block Diagram Representation of the Proposed System

The block diagram represents the high-level architecture and data flow within the Dione system, illustrating how user inputs are transformed into executed actions through a series of processing stages. The architecture follows a layered design pattern where each layer has a specific responsibility and communicates with adjacent layers through well-defined interfaces.

The User Interface Layer serves as the entry point for all user interactions. It accepts natural language input through either the CLI or the web-based GUI and displays results, progress updates, and error messages back to the user. The interface layer is responsible for parsing raw user input, formatting it for consumption by the reasoning layer, and presenting system outputs in a human-readable format. The Context Manager is the second processing stage, responsible for enriching the user's raw input with relevant contextual information retrieved from the system's memory. This contextual information is appended to the user's input before it is passed to the LLM, enabling the model to generate more accurate and personalized responses. The Context Manager also maintains the current conversation history, ensuring continuity across multiple turns of interaction.

The Local LLM (Reasoning Engine) is the core intelligence of the system. It receives the context-augmented input and generates structured outputs in the form of JSON commands that specify the actions to be performed. The Reasoning Engine operates entirely on the local device, using frameworks such as Ollama or Llama.cpp for model inference. It implements the ReAct loop, alternating between reasoning steps (where the model analyzes the situation and plans actions) and action steps (where the model generates specific tool calls). The engine supports multiple model backends and can be configured to use different models for different types of tasks.

The Validation Layer (Security Kernel) acts as a critical security checkpoint between the reasoning and execution stages. Every command generated by the LLM is validated against predefined schemas using Pydantic models to ensure structural correctness. The layer also checks commands against security policies, verifying that the requested operation is permitted, that all required parameters are present and valid, and that the command does not attempt any prohibited actions. High-risk operations, such as file deletion or system modification, trigger a confirmation request that requires explicit user approval before proceeding.

The Plugin Registry identifies and invokes the appropriate tool for executing each validated command. It maintains a registry of all available plugins, their capabilities, and their interfaces. When a command is received, the registry matches it to the appropriate plugin based on the specified tool name and function. The registry supports dynamic plugin loading, allowing new plugins to be added to the system without restarting or modifying the core engine.

The Execution Layer performs the actual actions specified by the validated commands. Each plugin operates in a sandboxed environment that isolates its execution from the core system and other plugins. The execution layer handles the communication between plugins and external systems such as email servers, messaging APIs, and the local file system. After each action is completed, the results are fed back into the Context Manager and the Reasoning Engine, enabling the ReAct loop to continue with the next step of the workflow.

4.4 Algorithms Utilized in Existing Systems

A comparative analysis of the algorithms and approaches used in existing AI assistant systems provides important context for understanding Dione's technical contributions. Traditional virtual assistants such as Siri, Alexa, and Google Assistant use Intent Classification Algorithms that map user input to a fixed set of predefined commands. These systems rely on supervised learning models trained on labeled datasets of user utterances and corresponding intents. While effective for a bounded set of supported commands, this approach cannot handle novel requests that fall outside the predefined intent taxonomy.

Sequence-to-Sequence Models form the foundation of modern LLMs and are used for text generation tasks including translation, summarization, and dialogue generation. These models use encoder-decoder architectures with attention mechanisms to generate output sequences conditioned on input sequences. While powerful for text generation, these models lack the ability to interact with external systems or execute actions in the real world.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning, introduced by Wei et al., enables language models to perform complex reasoning tasks by generating intermediate reasoning steps before producing a final answer. This technique significantly improves performance on mathematical, logical, and commonsense reasoning tasks. However, CoT reasoning is limited to generating text-based reasoning traces and does not include any mechanism for executing actions or interacting with external tools.

The ReAct Algorithm, which Dione adopts as its core operational paradigm, combines the strengths of chain-of-thought reasoning with the ability to take actions and observe results. By interleaving reasoning and acting, ReAct enables language models to dynamically adjust their approach based on real-world feedback, making it uniquely suited for agentic AI applications. Search and Retrieval Algorithms based on RAG are used for context enhancement by retrieving relevant information from external knowledge bases to supplement the model's parametric knowledge.

The fundamental limitation of existing systems is that they either focus on reasoning (generating text-based plans and instructions) or on execution (carrying out predefined workflows) but fail to integrate both capabilities securely within a local environment. Dione addresses this limitation by combining ReAct-based reasoning with a secure, plugin-based execution framework that operates entirely on the user's device.

These inputs range from simple, single-action commands such as "create a folder on the desktop" to complex, multi-step instructions such as "read my latest email, summarize it, and send a response." The diversity and unpredictability of user inputs represent a key challenge for the system, as it must be capable of understanding and acting on a virtually unlimited range of natural language expressions.

System context constitutes the second major data source, encompassing information about the current state of the user's computing environment. This includes the active window and application, the current working directory, recent file system changes, running processes, and system resource utilization. The system context provides crucial information that helps the LLM make informed decisions about how to execute requested tasks. For example, if a user asks to "save this file," the system context reveals which application and file are currently active, enabling the system to determine the appropriate save operation.

Local storage represents a rich repository of user data that the system can access and process in response to user requests. This includes documents in various formats such as PDF, DOCX, and TXT files, as well as images, spreadsheets, and other file types stored on the local file system. The system indexes and embedds relevant documents using the ChromaDB vector database, enabling efficient semantic search and retrieval when contextual information is needed.

External APIs provide access to cloud-based services such as Gmail for email operations, Google Calendar for scheduling, and messaging platforms such as WhatsApp for communication tasks. While these services require internet connectivity, all API communication is initiated and managed locally by Dione's plugins, ensuring that the user's credentials and data are handled entirely on the local device. The system uses OAuth 2.0 authentication flows that store tokens locally, never transmitting credentials to any third-party server other than the service provider itself.

The vector database powered by ChromaDB serves as both a data source and a data store, containing embeddings of past interactions, document summaries, and contextual information that has been accumulated over the course of the system's operation. This database enables the RAG-based context retrieval system that enriches user inputs with relevant historical information, improving the accuracy and relevance of the system's responses over time. Data is acquired dynamically at runtime, making the system adaptive and context-aware, eliminating the need for predefined datasets and allowing real-time decision-making based on the most current information available.

5.2 Data Preprocessing Techniques

Since the primary input to the Dione system is unstructured natural language text, preprocessing is essential for transforming raw user input into a format that enables accurate reasoning by the LLM and reliable execution by the plugin system. The preprocessing pipeline consists of multiple stages, each addressing a specific aspect of input transformation.

Text Normalization is the first preprocessing step, responsible for cleaning and standardizing raw user input. This includes correcting common spelling errors, expanding abbreviations and contractions, resolving ambiguous references using contextual clues, and normalizing whitespace and punctuation. The normalization process ensures that the LLM receives clean, consistent input regardless of variations in the user's typing style or language use. For example, inputs like "pls send msg to john abt meeting tmrw" are normalized to a more standard form before being processed by the LLM.

Tokenization converts the normalized input text into a sequence of tokens that can be processed by the LLM. The tokenization scheme must match the specific model being used, as different models employ different tokenization strategies such as Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), WordPiece, or SentencePiece. The system's Model Adapter module handles tokenization transparently, applying the correct tokenization scheme based on the active model configuration.

Context Augmentation through RAG is a critical preprocessing step that retrieves relevant information from the vector database and appends it to the user's input before sending it to the LLM. The retrieval process uses semantic similarity search to identify past interactions, stored documents, and system state information that are relevant to the current request. The retrieved context is formatted into a structured prompt template that clearly delineates the user's current input from the supplementary context, enabling the LLM to leverage the additional information without confusing it with the current request.

Schema Structuring converts the LLM's natural language outputs into structured JSON format that can be parsed and executed by the plugin system. The system uses carefully designed output format instructions in the LLM prompt to encourage the generation of well-formed JSON objects that specify the target plugin, function name, and parameters for each action. Post-processing logic handles cases where the LLM's output deviates from the expected format, applying pattern matching and correction heuristics to extract actionable commands from imperfect outputs.

Validation Preprocessing using Pydantic models provides the final preprocessing step before command execution. Each generated command is validated against a Pydantic schema that defines the expected structure, data types, and constraints for the command's parameters. This validation catches malformed commands, missing required parameters, out-of-range values, and type mismatches before they can cause errors during execution. Commands that fail validation are rejected with detailed error messages that are fed back to the LLM, prompting it to generate a corrected version. These preprocessing steps collectively ensure that user input is transformed into a format that is suitable for both reasoning and execution, significantly reducing errors and improving the overall reliability of the system.

5.4 Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation of Dione's performance uses a set of metrics specifically designed for agentic AI systems, focusing on the practical utility of the system rather than traditional machine learning metrics such as accuracy on benchmark datasets. These metrics were selected to assess the system's effectiveness across the key dimensions of task completion, responsiveness, efficiency, privacy, and safety.

The Task Success Rate (TSR) measures the percentage of user-initiated tasks that are successfully completed to the user's satisfaction. A task is considered successful if all required actions are executed correctly, the expected outputs are produced, and no unintended side effects occur. The TSR is calculated as the ratio of successfully completed tasks to total attempted tasks, expressed as a percentage. This metric provides a comprehensive measure of the system's end-to-end effectiveness, capturing failures at any stage of the pipeline including intent misunderstanding, incorrect action generation, validation failures, and execution errors.

Time-to-Action (TTA) measures the elapsed time from when the user submits an input to when the system begins executing the first action. This metric reflects the system's responsiveness and is critical for user satisfaction, as long delays between input and action create a perception of sluggishness. The TTA is influenced by factors including model inference speed, context retrieval latency, and validation processing time. The target TTA for simple single-step tasks is less than three seconds.

Token Generation Speed measures the rate at which the LLM generates output tokens, expressed in tokens per second. This metric directly reflects the inference performance of the local model and is heavily dependent on the hardware configuration, model size, and quantization level. Higher token generation speeds translate to faster response times and a more fluid user experience. The target for the system is a minimum of 10 tokens per second on the recommended hardware configuration.

The Privacy Metric quantifies the system's compliance with its privacy-first design principles by measuring the volume of data transmitted to external servers during operation. The target for this metric is zero unauthorized data leakage, meaning that no user data should be sent to any server other than the specific service endpoints required for task execution (such as Gmail's SMTP server for sending emails). The metric is verified through network traffic monitoring during test sessions.

The Safety Metric tracks the system's ability to prevent the execution of potentially unsafe or unauthorized commands. This includes commands that could delete important files, modify system configurations, send messages without authorization, or perform any other action that could have negative consequences. The target is one hundred percent prevention of unsafe commands that are not explicitly approved by the user through the confirmation mechanism.

CHAPTER 7: CONCLUSION

The development of Dione – a Local Large Action Model (LAM) Orchestration Engine – represents a significant advancement in the evolution of artificial intelligence systems, particularly in addressing the long-standing limitation known as the Actuation Gap. While modern Large Language Models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in reasoning, natural language understanding, and content generation, they remain fundamentally constrained by their inability to execute real-world actions. This project bridges that gap by introducing an intelligent orchestration layer that enables seamless interaction between AI reasoning and system-level execution, all within the privacy-preserving confines of the user's local device.

The core contribution of this project lies in its local-first architecture, which fundamentally redefines how agentic AI systems are designed and deployed. Unlike conventional cloud-based approaches that require users to expose sensitive data to external servers for processing, Dione ensures that all reasoning, context handling, and decision-making occur entirely on the user's device. This architectural choice guarantees data sovereignty, enhanced privacy, and reduced security risks, making the system particularly suitable for applications involving confidential or sensitive information in domains such as healthcare, finance, legal services, and personal productivity. By eliminating dependency on remote servers, the system also improves reliability by functioning without internet connectivity for local tasks, and reduces latency by avoiding network round-trip times, thereby enhancing the overall user experience.

From a technical perspective, the system integrates several advanced concepts into a cohesive and functional architecture. The ReAct (Reason + Act) loop serves as the core operational paradigm, enabling iterative reasoning and action execution that allows the system to tackle complex, multi-step workflows dynamically. The Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system, powered by ChromaDB, provides contextual memory that improves the accuracy and relevance of the system's responses over time. The modular plugin-based execution framework ensures scalability and extensibility, allowing new functionalities to be added through plugins without modifying the core system. The Safety Guardrail module provides multi-layered security controls that validate all actions before execution, ensuring that the system operates safely and in accordance with user-defined policies.

The project has successfully demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed approach through working implementations of several key plugins, including file system operations, email management through Gmail, and messaging through WhatsApp. These demonstrations validate that it is possible to build a fully functional agentic AI system that operates locally, handles real-world tasks autonomously, and maintains the highest standards of privacy and security. The gap analysis presented in this report confirms that Dione addresses multiple fundamental limitations of existing cloud-based AI systems, positioning it as a significant contribution to the field of agentic AI.

Looking forward, the project has laid a strong foundation for the next phase of development, which will focus on expanding the plugin ecosystem, optimizing performance, enhancing security mechanisms, and developing a user-friendly graphical interface. The completion of these planned activities will result in a comprehensive, production-ready system that demonstrates the full potential of local, privacy-preserving agentic AI. Dione represents not just a technical achievement, but a step toward a future where AI systems empower users with intelligent automation while respecting their fundamental right to privacy and control over their personal data.
